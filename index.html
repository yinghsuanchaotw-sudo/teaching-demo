North Carolina’s UI Reform

What happens when unemployment insurance becomes less generous? Understand North Carolina’s 2013 reform, then use difference-in-differences to evaluate changes in unemployment.

Policy background · North Carolina, 2013

What changed—and why?

Unemployment insurance (UI) provides temporary income support to eligible workers who lose their jobs. It helps workers maintain spending while searching for work, but its generosity may also affect how quickly they accept jobs or whether they continue searching.

Following the Great Recession, North Carolina’s unemployment insurance system owed substantial debt to the federal government. In February 2013, the state enacted a reform intended to address that debt and change its UI program. The legislation combined benefit reductions with changes to employer financing.

Key dates

  • February 19, 2013: the legislation was signed into law. Workers and employers could learn about the coming changes before implementation.
  • July 1, 2013: the new state benefit rules took effect for claims filed on or after this date.
  • End of June 2013: federal Emergency Unemployment Compensation (EUC) ended in NC, removing additional federal support for long-term unemployed workers.

What did the benefit changes involve?

ComponentChange
Weekly benefit amountA new maximum of $350 per week, together with a revised calculation based on prior earnings. $350 was a cap, not the amount every claimant received.
Regular benefit durationThe previous maximum of 26 weeks was replaced by an unemployment-rate-linked schedule with maximum durations ranging from 12 to 20 weeks. An individual’s entitlement also depended on eligibility and prior earnings.
Federal emergency benefitsNC lost access to EUC because the state benefit reductions did not satisfy the federal program’s maintenance-of-benefits requirement. Other eligible states continued receiving EUC until the program expired nationally at the end of 2013.

What are we evaluating?

Our question is: Did NC’s unemployment rate change differently after the reform than it would have without the reform? We begin with Virginia as a neighboring comparison state, then broaden the comparison group.

The outcome is the seasonally adjusted state unemployment rate. We evaluate the combined policy change; this design cannot separately identify the effects of smaller weekly payments, shorter regular benefits, and the loss of federal emergency benefits.

Before estimating: a fall in unemployment is not enough to establish a policy effect. The economy was also recovering. And unemployment can fall when people leave the labor force, not only when they find jobs.

Policy sources: Session Law 2013-2; U.S. Department of Labor explanation of EUC eligibility; NC Department of Commerce, December 2013 activity report; NC UI debt background.

Our method: Difference-in-Differences

Compare the change in NC with the change in a comparison state. The comparison state’s change helps construct a counterfactual: what NC’s unemployment rate might have been without the reform. A causal interpretation requires that, without the reform, the groups would have experienced the same average change.

Your task

Start with one treated state, one comparison state, and two periods.

  1. Calculate. Compare NC and Virginia in July–December 2012 and July–December 2013. Complete the worksheet’s before-and-after table.
  2. Estimate. Run the basic interaction regression. Explain why its DiD coefficient matches your calculation.
  3. Inspect. Plot monthly unemployment trends and assess whether the comparison is convincing.
  4. Extend. Expand the pre-period and comparison group, then investigate policy timing.
  5. Interpret. Write a 150–200-word policy memo explaining the estimate and the assumptions behind it.

The outcome is the unemployment rate—not UI receipt duration. A lower unemployment rate does not, on its own, establish an employment or welfare gain.
